Abstract
Transient stability assessment (TSA) requires predicting whether a power system remains synchronized after a large disturbance and estimating the critical clearing time (CCT) of faults. Time-domain simulation (TDS) is accurate but too slow for online screening, whereas existing machine-learning surrogates are fast but output only a stable/unstable label, discard the rotor-angle trajectory, and provide no error bound. We introduce a physics-informed neural operator that maps a fault scenario to the full center-of-inertia rotor-angle trajectory and attach a distribution-free, finite-sample error bound to the trajectory through split conformal prediction. The key enabler is a physics-informed input encoding: a fault is represented by the vector of initial accelerations it induces on each machine, computed from the swing equation, rather than by an arbitrary bus index. This encoding allows the operator to generalize to fault locations and to N−1 and N−2 topologies not seen during training. On the IEEE 39-bus test system, the operator runs about 700× faster than TDS on identical CPU hardware, estimates the CCT to within 12.3 ms on average, and classifies stability with 96.7% accuracy. Its CCT accuracy is comparable to that of the best classical direct criterion tested (an IMEAC-style criterion, 9.6 ms; PEBS, 18.7 ms), while additionally returning the trajectory and a calibrated bound. It transfers to the IEEE 118-bus system (95.0% classification accuracy) and to one-axis, exciter, and damped generator models with comparable accuracy. We openly report trajectory accuracy together with several negative results: a physics-residual loss does not improve on the physics-informed encoding, and generalization is currently limited to fault locations and topologies, as operating-point (load-level) generalization remains open. To our knowledge, this is the first work to couple a trajectory-level operator with conformal certification for rotor-angle trajectories and CCT extraction in TSA.
Keywords: transient stability; neural operator; physics-informed machine learning; conformal prediction; critical clearing time; power system dynamics
1. Introduction
Transient stability is the ability of a power system to remain in synchronism after a large disturbance such as a three-phase fault. Its assessment is a safety-critical, recurring task: for a given contingency and fault clearing time, system operators must know whether the system survives and, ideally, how much clearing-time margin remains before it loses synchronism [1, 2]. The standard tool is time-domain simulation (TDS), which integrates the nonlinear swing equations and is accurate but computationally expensive, particularly when screening many fault locations, clearing times, and topology changes [3].
This cost has motivated a large body of machine-learning surrogates for TSA. Most learn a direct map from operating-condition or fault features to a binary stable/unstable label using classifiers such as support vector machines, decision trees, or deep networks [4]. Fast as they are, these surrogates have three limitations that matter for deployment. First, they discard the rotor-angle trajectory and thus cannot provide a stability margin or a CCT estimate. Second, they do not generalize naturally across fault locations: representing a fault by a one-hot bus index yields no signal for unseen buses. Third, and most importantly for a safety-critical application, they output no uncertainty or error bound, so an operator cannot know when the surrogate is trustworthy.
We address all three limitations with a physics-informed neural operator equipped with conformal certification. The operator is a Deep Operator Network (DeepONet) [5], whose branch network encodes a fault scenario and whose Fourier-feature trunk network encodes time; their contraction yields the full center-of-inertia (COI) rotor-angle trajectory rather than merely a label. The fault scenario is encoded by a physics-informed vector: the initial acceleration (Pmi − Peif)/Mi that the fault induces on each machine, computed directly from the swing equation. This continuous, physically meaningful encoding is what allows the operator to generalize across unseen fault locations. Finally, we use split conformal prediction [6] to attach a finite-sample, distribution-free error bound to every predicted trajectory, together with a tighter score that directly bounds the pairwise angle difference entering the stability criterion.
Our contributions are as follows.
We couple, to our knowledge for the first time, a trajectory-level neural operator with conformal certification for rotor-angle trajectories and CCT estimation. Prior neural operators for power systems target component-level dynamics [7]; conformalized operator regression has appeared for post-fault bus-voltage trajectories [8], targeting voltage rather than rotor-angle stability and without CCT extraction; and prior conformal work on TSA predicts only a binary stability label [9]. None predicts the rotor-angle trajectory or the CCT with a conformal bound.
We show that a physics-informed input encoding—the swing-equation acceleration vector—is what enables generalization across unseen fault locations, and we quantify the difference against a one-hot encoding and against graph baselines using raw observables.
We provide a conformal certification layer that yields a finite-sample, distribution-free error bound on the trajectory and a directly calibrated pairwise-angle bound that is informative for the stability decision at the 90% target level, together with a CCT estimate of comparable accuracy to classical direct criteria.
We extend the encoding with a post-fault severity vector and show that a single operator generalizes to N−1 and N−2 line-trip topologies not seen in training.
We openly report trajectory accuracy together with an ablation of an auxiliary physics-residual loss, including a negative result: at the mildest weight the residual term gives no improvement over the physics-informed encoding, and at larger weights it degrades accuracy, so that for this problem physics is injected more reliably through the input encoding than through a residual loss.
2. Related Work
2.1. Neural operators and physics-informed learning in power systems
Neural operators learn maps between function spaces rather than between fixed-size vectors. The two dominant architectures are DeepONet [5], which represents an operator as a contraction of a branch network (encoding the input function) with a trunk network (encoding the output coordinates), and the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) [10], which performs learned linear transforms in the Fourier domain. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) [11] embed governing-equation residuals into the training loss to regularize the model toward physically consistent solutions.
Recent work has begun applying these tools to power systems. Karampinis et al. [7] propose a DeepONet-based framework for modeling power-system components with physics-informed variants, targeting fast dynamic simulation and digital twins, but do not address transient stability assessment, the CCT, or uncertainty quantification. For operator learning specifically, Moya et al. [12] attach split-conformal intervals to DeepONets (Conformalized-DeepONet), yielding distribution-free coverage for operator regression on canonical dynamical systems; our work instead targets the post-fault power-system trajectory and the CCT. A line of physics-informed graphical and neural-network surrogates has been applied to power flow and optimal power flow [13, 14], focusing on steady-state problems rather than post-fault dynamics. Our work differs in targeting the post-fault rotor-angle trajectory operator and in coupling it with conformal certification.
2.2. Learning-based transient stability assessment
Learning-based TSA has long been dominated by classifiers that predict a stable/unstable label from hand-crafted features such as voltage magnitudes, active power, or rotor-angle snapshots [4]. More recent work uses graph neural networks to exploit the network topology and deep sequence models for the post-fault time series. Modern graph models with distribution-aware pooling [15] and related designs report high classification accuracies on the IEEE 39-bus system under in-sample or per-sample test protocols. Under our held-out-bus protocol with the same raw features, however, a standard graph convolutional classifier reaches only 76.0%, and a stronger residual graph model 77.3% (Section 6), and graph models produce no trajectory, CCT, or uncertainty bound.
A small number of works predict the post-fault trajectory rather than only the label; these are closest in spirit to ours, but they provide no error bounds. Event-structured PINNs (ES-PINN) model the pre-fault, fault-on, and post-fault stages explicitly with hard state chaining and a differentiable CCT boundary [16]; their residual-to-trajectory-to-CCT error theorem is complementary to our conformal certification, which provides a finite-sample error bound rather than a theoretical error-propagation analysis. PINN-based acceleration of time-domain simulation [17] solves the governing equations through residual losses at inference time; our operator instead learns the scenario-to-trajectory map with a physics-informed input encoding, avoiding per-scenario residual minimization. Trajectory-sensitivity methods [18] compute the CCT analytically from linearized sensitivities under small-perturbation assumptions; our CCT comes from the learned operator and is validated against TDS.
Confidence-aware TSA has begun to emerge. Lu et al. [9] combine a large language model with conformal prediction to produce prediction sets for a binary stability label, but they predict neither the trajectory nor the CCT and do not use a physics-informed operator. Conformalized operator regression has also appeared for post-fault bus-voltage trajectories [8]: there the target is voltage-interval coverage for voltage stability, calibrated per output with quantile heads. Our target is instead rotor-angle trajectory coverage for the π-separation and CCT criteria, calibrated with a worst-case trajectory score (and a directly calibrated pairwise score, Section 4.4). Probabilistic and Bayesian operator frameworks for post-fault trajectories (DeepONet-grid-UQ) [19] quantify uncertainty through Gaussian or posterior-sampling assumptions; our split-conformal intervals are distribution-free with finite-sample guarantees. Risk-controlling quantile operators (UQNO) [23] learn a second operator for pointwise uncertainty radii with a PAC-style functional calibration; our bands require only a calibration set on top of the trained operator.
Classical direct methods remain strong CCT baselines. The transient-energy-function (TEF/PEBS) method [20] and the individual-machine equal-area criterion (IMEAC) [21] extract the CCT from machine-level energy criteria; Section 6 compares our CCT against both. Our contribution is the combination of a trajectory-level neural operator with a physics-informed fault encoding and a conformal error bound, which yields a conformal CCT estimate alongside the trajectory.
3. Problem Formulation
3.1. Classical multi-machine model
We consider the classical model of transient stability, in which each synchronous generator is represented as a constant voltage source Ei behind a transient reactance x′di [1, 2]. Under the standard reduction, loads are converted to constant admittances and the network is Kron-reduced to the internal generator nodes, yielding an n×n admittance matrix Y. The dynamics of generator i obey the swing equations
	dδi/dt = ωs(ωi − 1),	(1)
	dωi/dt = [Pmi − Pei(δ) − Di(ωi − 1)] / (2Hi),	(2)
where δi is the rotor angle, ωi the per-unit speed, Hi the inertia constant, Di the damping coefficient, Pmi the mechanical power, and ωs the synchronous speed. The electrical power is
	Pei(δ) = Σj=1n Ei Ej [ Gij cos(δi−δj) + Bij sin(δi−δj) ],	(3)
with Gij = Re{Yij} and Bij = Im{Yij}. We use the undamped case Di = 0, which is standard for the classical model and gives the undamped (energy-margin) stability boundary; a damped variant is examined in Section 6. Because the equations depend only on angle differences, it is convenient to work in the center-of-inertia (COI) frame, defining
	δ̃i = δi − δCOI,　δCOI = (Σi Mi δi)/(Σi Mi),　Mi = 2Hi.	(4)
A three-phase fault at a bus is modeled by adding a large shunt admittance to that bus and reducing the network during the fault; the fault is cleared after a clearing time tc, optionally tripping a line (an N−1 contingency). Reference trajectories are obtained by integrating (1)–(2) with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme at a 0.5 ms step. A trajectory is stable if the maximum pairwise angle difference remains below π over the simulation horizon—the π-separation criterion.
3.2. Problem statement
Let a fault scenario be described by a fault location (bus), an optional set of tripped lines, and a clearing time tc. We seek a map
	G: (scenario) ↦ { δ̃i(t) }i=1n,　t ∈ [0, T],	(5)
that returns the COI-referenced rotor-angle trajectory, together with (i) a stability classification, (ii) the critical clearing time tCCT (the largest tc for which the trajectory is stable), and (iii) a certified error bound on the trajectory.
4. Method
4.1. Physics-informed fault encoding
A central obstacle to generalization across fault locations is that a bus index is an arbitrary label with no physical meaning: replacing it with a one-hot vector gives the branch network no information about physically similar buses. We instead encode a fault at bus k by the vector of initial accelerations it induces, computed from the swing equation at the pre-fault equilibrium,
	ai(k) = [Pmi − Peif(k)] / Mi,	(6)
where Peif(k) is the electrical power of machine i during the fault at bus k, evaluated at the pre-fault angles. This vector is precisely the initial rate of change of speed in (2) and is the dominant driver of the fault-on transient. It is continuous in the fault admittance, so physically similar faults map to nearby encodings. The branch input is the concatenation of a(k) ∈ ℝn and the normalized clearing time.
For N−1 contingencies, in which a line is tripped at fault clearing, the post-fault reduced admittance and the post-fault dynamics both change. We encode this change symmetrically with a post-fault severity vector,
	ãi(l) = [Pmi − Peipost(l)] / Mi,	(7)
where Peipost(l) is the post-fault electrical power at the pre-fault angles with line l removed; for the intact network ãi = 0. The full scenario encoding is the concatenation of the fault-on severity (6), the post-fault severity (7), and the clearing time. For N−2 contingencies the same vector is evaluated from the admittance matrix with both lines removed: because ã is always an n-dimensional vector evaluated at the pre-fault angles, the encoding dimension is unchanged for any number of tripped lines, and the same branch network applies without modification. Both severity vectors are continuous in the fault admittance, so physically similar faults and topologies map to nearby encodings.
4.2. Neural operator architecture
We use a DeepONet. The branch network bθ maps the scenario encoding to a matrix of basis coefficients B ∈ ℝn×p; the trunk network τφ maps time to p basis functions. The predicted trajectory is the contraction
	δ̂(s,t) = B(s) τ(t) ∈ ℝn.	(8)
Because the swing dynamics are oscillatory, we feed the trunk a Fourier feature embedding of time, {cos(2πkt/T), sin(2πkt/T)}k=1K, before a small multilayer perceptron. This lets the trunk represent the natural oscillation modes far more efficiently than a plain multilayer perceptron. The trunk is smooth in t, so its time derivatives are available by automatic differentiation, which we use for the optional physics residual below. Fig. 1 summarizes the proposed pipeline.

4.3. Physics-residual loss (auxiliary)
Following the PINN paradigm, we optionally add a residual of the post-fault swing equation in the COI frame. Differentiating the COI definition (4) under Di = 0 gives Mi δ̃̈i = ωs(Pmi − Pei) − Mi ωs c(t), where c(t) = Σj(Pmj − Pej)/Mtot is the COI acceleration and Mtot = Σj Mj. Dividing by Mi so that machines of widely different inertia contribute comparably (otherwise the H = 500 s aggregate machine at bus 39 dominates the sum), the per-machine residual enforced at a set C of collocation times in the post-fault region t ≥ tc is
	Lphys = (1/|C|) Σt∈C, i [ δ̃̈i − ωs(Pmi − Pei(δ̃))/Mi + ωs c(t) ]².	(9)
Two aspects warrant clarification. First, the non-dimensionalization: the residual is divided by Mi before squaring, yielding a per-machine acceleration residual comparable across machines. Second, the role of ωs: it enters through the frequency-to-angle relation dδi/dt = ωs(ωi − 1) and therefore multiplies the power mismatch; it fixes the relative scaling between the curvature and power-mismatch terms and cannot be omitted. The total training loss is the data loss plus λLphys. The residual is enforced only in the post-fault region because the fault-on dynamics use the fault-dependent reduced admittance, which varies per scenario; collocation points are every fifth output time point (a 0.05 s stride), and denser collocation is ablated in Section 6.
4.4. Conformal certification
The trajectory operator alone gives no indication of its own error. We use split conformal prediction [6] to attach a finite-sample, distribution-free bound to the single-machine COI angles. Because the trajectory is only well-posed in the stable region (unstable angles diverge), we calibrate on stable scenarios. For a scenario s, the nonconformity score is the worst-case prediction error over machines and time,
	R(s) = maxi,t | δ̂̃i(t;s) − δ̃i(t;s) |.	(10)
We split the N = 355 stable scenarios of the held-out test set (37% of the 960 held-out scenarios; the full dataset is ≈40% stable, Section 5)—which the operator never saw during training and which are divided at random from the same held-out-bus sampling distribution, so that calibration and evaluation scores are exchangeable—into a calibration half (Ncal = 177) and a disjoint evaluation half (Neva = 178). For a target miscoverage level α, the bound q̂ is the empirical (1−α) quantile of the calibration scores with the standard finite-sample correction. For a new exchangeable scenario, the whole predicted trajectory is then covered simultaneously:
	Pr{ maxi,t | δ̂̃i(t) − δ̃i(t) | ≤ q̂ } ≥ 1 − α,
i.e., with probability at least 1−α over scenarios, every single-machine COI angle at every time point lies within q̂ of the truth (the empirical scenario-level coverage is 0.893 at α = 0.1). By the triangle inequality, the pairwise angle difference—the quantity relevant to the stability criterion—is bounded by 2q̂. At α = 0.1 this pairwise bound is 2q̂ = 222°, which exceeds the π-separation threshold: the per-machine envelope therefore does not certify the stability decision at that level and becomes informative for the criterion only at larger miscoverage (e.g., 2q̂ = 81° at α = 0.5).
To bound the decision quantity directly, we also calibrate a pairwise-difference score,
	Rpw(s) = maxi,j,t | êi(t) − êj(t) |,　êi(t) = δ̂̃i(t) − δ̃i(t),	(11)
which scores exactly the pairwise angle spread appearing in the π-separation criterion. As shown in Section 6, Rpw certifies the pairwise difference to 102° at α = 0.1 (empirical coverage 0.893), below the π threshold, and is therefore informative for the stability decision at the nominal level. Both bounds are conservative: the per-machine half-width q̂ (111° at α = 0.1) is substantially larger than the typical error because the worst-case score covers near-boundary scenarios where the trajectory is inherently sensitive. The conformal layer is thus a safe screening envelope rather than a tight prediction or a stability certificate. We further derive a CCT band from the per-machine interval, but note that it is heuristic: because the CCT is a nonlinear functional of the trajectory, it does not inherit a formal coverage guarantee from the trajectory bound.
4.5. Critical clearing time estimation
Given the operator, the CCT of a fault is estimated by binary search over the clearing time: for each candidate tc, the operator predicts the trajectory, and the scenario is declared unstable if the maximum pairwise angle spread ever exceeds π, and stable otherwise. The search uses 16 iterations over [0.01, 0.8] s for the 39-bus system, shrinking the interval by 216 to (0.8−0.01)/216 ≈ 12 µs; the same protocol (with a system-dependent interval—[0.002, 0.3] s for the 118-bus system, whose CCTs are much shorter〖V4〗) is applied to the TDS reference, so the two CCT estimates are directly comparable. The same search, carried out with the pairwise bound 2q̂ defined above, yields a heuristic CCT band.
5. Experimental Setup
5.1. Test system and data
We use the IEEE 39-bus New England system with 10 machines, whose static data are the standard published values and whose dynamic parameters (H, x′d) are the canonical classical-model values [20, 1]. The reference simulator integrates (1)–(2) with RK4 at a 0.5 ms step over a 4 s horizon. We sample fault scenarios by drawing a fault bus uniformly over all 39 buses and a clearing time uniformly in [0.03, 0.55] s, producing a balanced mix of stable and unstable cases (approximately 40% stable). The dataset has 2160 training, 540 validation, and 960 test scenarios (80 scenarios per bus on the 27 training buses for both training and validation, and 80 per bus on the test buses). The test set is constructed from 12 fault buses held out entirely from training, so that test performance measures generalization to unseen fault locations. The CCT search range [0.01, 0.8] s covers the observed 39-bus CCTs〖V1〗; any CCT above the sampled clearing-time range would be an extrapolation and is reported as such.
5.2. Baselines and ablations
We compare against (i) the same DeepONet with a one-hot fault encoding, an ablation isolating the effect of the physics-informed encoding; (ii) a pointwise multilayer perceptron baseline that maps (scenario, time) to the angle directly; (iii) the operator trained with the auxiliary physics-residual loss at several weights; (iv) a graph convolutional network (GCN) that predicts the stability label from raw node features (voltage magnitudes and angles, loads, and a fault flag) and, in a regression variant, predicts the CCT directly; (v) a DeepONet with a self-attention branch; (vi) a Fourier-neural-operator trunk; and (vii) a recurrent (GRU) sequence-to-sequence baseline. We additionally compare the CCT against two classical direct methods: the transient-energy-function (PEBS) method [20] and an IMEAC-style individual-machine criterion [21]. All neural models share the same data, optimizer, and class-balanced mean-squared loss; the loss target saturates beyond ±3π so that the diverging unstable trajectories do not dominate the fit.
5.3. Evaluation
We report the root-mean-square error (RMSE) of the predicted trajectory over stable test scenarios, decomposed into the fault-on (t < tc) and post-fault (t ≥ tc) regions; the stability classification accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 (for the stable class); the conformal interval width and its empirical coverage; the CCT error against a brute-force TDS binary search; and the wall-clock speed-up of the operator over the RK4 reference. Throughout, "RMSE" refers to the pooled RMSE, i.e., the square root of the mean squared error over all stable scenarios, machines, and time steps. The pooled RMSE is computed over all points in one pass and equals the square root of the sample-size-weighted mean of the squared regional RMSEs—it is not a linear (time-weighted) average of the regional RMSEs; it is dominated by near-boundary scenarios and is therefore larger than a per-scenario time-averaged error.
The operator is implemented in PyTorch and trained on a single NVIDIA RTX 4070 GPU. Both networks use tanh activations: the branch has three hidden layers of width 512 and outputs n×p basis coefficients with p = 512; the trunk has three hidden layers of width 128 on top of K = 16 Fourier harmonics. We train with Adam (learning rate 10⁻³, cosine schedule, 600 epochs, batch size 128). The main results (Table 2) are for the seed-0 model; across four training seeds the classification accuracy is 96.7% ± 0.3% and the per-scenario mean RMSE is 20.2° ± 1.4°, so the reported values are representative. All test metrics other than conformal coverage (RMSE, classification, CCT) are computed on the full 960-scenario held-out set; only conformal coverage is restricted to a disjoint evaluation half (Section 4.4). The results are not artifacts of the hyperparameters: halving the Fourier harmonics to K = 8 degrades the pooled RMSE to 45.9° (95.1% accuracy). Reducing the loss saturation to ±2π leaves accuracy at 96.7% with a slightly better pooled RMSE of 34.3°; we retain ±3π as the default because it is selected on validation data and gives more stable fits to diverging unstable trajectories〖V5〗.
The speed-up is measured as per-scenario wall-clock time against the vectorized RK4 reference (0.5 ms step, 4 s horizon) after warm-up; both methods are single-threaded, vectorized implementations, so the comparison does not benefit from multi-core parallelism on either side. The operator is about 700× faster on identical CPU hardware, and GPU inference on the RTX 4070 raises this to about 1300× (Table 1). Production TDS tools typically use variable step sizes, multithreading, and richer models, so absolute timings vary by implementation, but the order-of-magnitude gap persists under like-for-like settings. The RK4 cost scales inversely with the reference step size, whereas the operator’s cost depends only on the number of output time points at which the trunk is evaluated (401 per trajectory here), not on the integration step, so the speed-up grows for finer reference steps. The CCT estimate requires a 16-iteration binary search (16 operator inferences, ≈0.5 ms on CPU), versus ≈373 ms for a brute-force TDS CCT search on the same hardware—a ≈750× speed-up for CCT estimation. The physics-informed encoding is computed once per fault: on the 39-bus system the Kron reduction of the fault-on network costs ≈0.13 ms per bus on CPU (roughly 4× a single operator inference), while on the 118-bus system it costs ≈4.1 ms; the cost is amortized over the clearing-time sweep, leaving the 39-bus speed-up essentially unchanged (a 100-point clearing-time sweep per fault costs 3.2 ms end-to-end, including the encoding, versus 2.3 s for RK4). The 118-bus end-to-end timing is reported separately in Section 6〖V9〗; factorized-update schemes can reduce the encoding cost further on very large grids. Hardware models, software versions, and the number of timing repetitions are listed in the reproducibility appendix〖V9〗.

6. Results
6.1. Trajectory prediction and classification
Table 2 summarizes the main results. The physics-informed operator predicts the fault-on segment of the trajectory to 2.3° RMSE—the segment most sensitive to fault-specific dynamics—and the post-fault segment to 36.8° RMSE. The post-fault error is concentrated near the stability boundary, where the trajectory is inherently sensitive; this sensitivity is exactly what the conformal bound is designed to capture. Decomposing the post-fault error confirms the mechanism: the first-swing window (up to the latest per-machine first-swing peak) is predicted to 10.5° pooled RMSE, while the subsequent oscillation carries 41.8°. The operator classifies stability with 96.7% accuracy (F1 = 0.95) and estimates the CCT to within 12.3 ms on average, at a ≈700× speed-up over the RK4 reference on identical CPU hardware. The CCT estimate is accurate despite the post-fault trajectory error because the CCT is determined by the first-swing peak angle, which the operator captures far more reliably than the subsequent oscillation; the conformal bound certifies the residual error rather than claiming it is small.
For reference, the transient-energy-function (PEBS) approach of Athay et al. [20] attains a mean CCT error of 18.7 ms with a conservative (under-estimating) bias on the same held-out buses, and an IMEAC-style individual-machine criterion [21]—judging stability per machine by the existence of a dynamic stationary point on the post-fault trajectory, evaluated on the same 12 held-out buses with the same 16-iteration binary search (1 ms step) as the TDS reference—yields CCTs within 9.6 ms of the pairwise-π reference. The operator’s 12.3 ms error is thus comparable to the best classical criterion (IMEAC) and smaller than PEBS, while additionally returning the full trajectory and a conformal bound without any fault-on or post-fault integration; the classical criteria agree closely with each other. Fig. 2 shows predicted versus true COI trajectories for a representative near-boundary scenario, and Fig. 3 plots predicted against true CCT across the held-out fault buses.

6.2. Effect of the physics-informed encoding
Table 3 reports the ablation on the fault encoding. Replacing the physics-informed acceleration vector with a one-hot bus index degrades the stable-trajectory RMSE from 36.2° to 141.0° and the classification accuracy from 96.7% to 88.7%, because a one-hot encoding provides no signal for unseen fault buses. The one-hot baseline also overfits at p = 512: its RMSE exceeds the 104° RMSE of predictions uniformly distributed over the ±180° angle range (180°/√3 ≈ 104°), and reducing its capacity does not recover meaningful generalization. This confirms that the physics-informed encoding is the mechanism enabling generalization across fault locations (Fig. 4).
A GCN classifier—a standard topology-aware TSA baseline—operating on raw node features (voltage magnitudes and angles, loads, and a fault flag) achieves only 76.0% classification accuracy, versus 96.7% for the severity-encoded operator. This comparison is not architecture-controlled: both the input features and the task differ (the GCN outputs a label, the operator a trajectory). It nevertheless shows that the severity encoding carries far more information than raw observables for this task, in addition to enabling generalization across fault locations. A stronger graph baseline with three residual graph layers and concatenated mean/max/sum pooling attains 77.3% ± 3.9% (five seeds) on the same raw features, indicating that the gap is not an artifact of a weak graph architecture. A second graph baseline, a GCN that regresses the CCT directly from the same raw node features (trained on the 27 training fault buses, evaluated on the 12 held-out buses, five seeds), attains a mean CCT error of 48.0 ms (max 224.4 ms), versus 12.3 ms (max 48.8 ms) for the severity-encoded operator; the roughly 4× larger CCT error confirms that the physics-informed encoding, not the graph structure alone, is what generalizes the CCT to unseen fault locations. Neither graph baseline produces a trajectory or a conformal error bound. A recurrent (GRU) sequence-to-sequence baseline, mapping the same severity encoding to the trajectory through a sequential decoder rather than an operator contraction, attains 57.4° RMSE and 94.0% accuracy (Table 3), worse than the operator’s 36.2°: the low-rank operator structure outperforms a generic recurrent decoder on this problem.

6.3. Generalization to N−1 and N−2 topologies
We train a single operator (p = 512) on a mix of N−0 and N−1 scenarios (line trips drawn from 25 training lines; the training set contains 〖V7：多拓扑训练集配比与总场景数待补〗 scenarios) using the extended encoding of Section 4, and evaluate generalization along two held-out axes: unseen fault buses and unseen trip lines (10 lines). Table 4 reports the results. On held-out line trips the operator achieves 31.7° stable-trajectory RMSE and 96.1% classification accuracy, demonstrating that the post-fault severity encoding lets the operator generalize to topologies never seen in training. On held-out fault buses the RMSE is 63.9° and the accuracy 94.7%, reflecting the harder combined task; for reference, the N−0-only operator attains 36.2° on the corresponding held-out fault buses, so the multi-topology operator trades some N−0 accuracy for topology generalization, which we report openly.
The same encoding extends to N−2 contingencies (two lines tripped): on held-out line pairs the operator attains 43.2° stable-trajectory RMSE and 93.1% classification accuracy (Table 4), with no architecture change, because the post-fault severity vector is simply evaluated from the admittance matrix with both lines removed. The multi-topology operator also estimates the CCT on held-out combinations: a mean error of 17.1 ms on unseen trip lines, 29.3 ms on held-out fault buses (Table 4), and 31.8 ms on held-out N−2 line pairs. The N−2 evaluation comprises 863 held-out bus–line-pair combinations 〖V7：补 863 的算式，例如 held-out 故障母线数 × 线路对组合数〗; split-conformal coverage on these splits is reported in Section 6.6.

6.4. Sample efficiency of the operator
A motivation for the operator architecture is its low-rank inductive bias. We verify this with a data-efficiency experiment, training the DeepONet and the pointwise MLP baseline on 10%, 25%, 50%, and 100% of the training scenarios (three seeds each) and evaluating on the full held-out test set. At 10% of the data the operator attains 56.1° RMSE versus 65.2° for the MLP (a ≈9° advantage); at 25% the gap is ≈8° (46.4° versus 54.8°); at 50% and 100% the gap narrows to ≈2–3° (49.9° versus 51.6°, and 38.8° versus 41.5°). The non-monotonicity at intermediate fractions is within the across-seed standard deviation〖V11：补三次种子的 ± 标准差〗. The compact low-rank structure therefore confers a modest but consistent sample-efficiency advantage in the low-data regime, precisely where fast TSA surrogates are most valuable. The sample-efficiency numbers use the base p = 128 architecture averaged over three seeds, whereas Table 3 reports the p = 512 architecture, which explains the different full-data values (38.8°/41.5° here versus 36.2°/43.1° in the main experiment).
6.5. Extensions to higher-fidelity generator models and damping
The classical model treats each machine as a constant voltage behind a transient reactance. To verify that our approach transfers to a more realistic generator representation, we repeat the experiment with the one-axis (flux-decay) model, in which the q-axis transient EMF E′q obeys dynamics driven by the field voltage and the d-axis armature reaction (a third-order per-machine model [1, Ch. 8]). The data protocol is identical to the classical case—the same 12 held-out fault buses, the same clearing-time range, and the same p = 512 architecture—so only the ground-truth trajectories change. The severity encoding is unchanged; the one-axis model yields lower critical clearing times than the classical model, as expected from flux decay. On this model the operator attains 38.6° pooled stable-trajectory RMSE (1.8° fault-on, 39.2° post-fault), 97.1% classification accuracy, a CCT error of ≈13 ms, and 90% conformal coverage (α = 0.1), all comparable to or better than the classical-model results.
Adding a first-order automatic voltage regulator (a simplified IEEE DC1A exciter with KA = 20, TA = 0.2 s and rate limits; see IEEE Std 421.5) yields a fourth-order per-machine model whose critical clearing times are lower than under constant field voltage (a known negative-damping effect). On this exciter model the operator attains 25.2° pooled stable-trajectory RMSE (2.2° fault-on, 25.5° post-fault), 94.0% classification accuracy, and 90% conformal coverage—the lowest absolute RMSE of the three generator models, largely because voltage regulation smooths the post-fault oscillation. The exciter model has sparser stable data, so the larger p = 512 network overfits it slightly; at p = 128 it attains 19.7°.
We also probe damped dynamics: adding uniform damping D = 1 to the classical swing equations leaves the severity encoding unchanged—it is evaluated at the pre-fault equilibrium, where the damping term vanishes—so the same operator transfers without modification. The fault-on region improves to 1.8° pooled RMSE, but the post-fault pooled RMSE rises to 47.3° (versus 36.8° undamped) and classification drops to 94.8% (CCT error 17.4 ms). Near-boundary damped trajectories decay slowly and linger at high angle spreads, so small errors in the predicted decay rate accumulate over the 4 s window and produce a heavier error tail (maximum worst-case error 638°). The split-conformal interval remains approximately valid (mean coverage 0.92 over repeated calibration splits〖V11：补均值 ± 标准差与重复划分次数〗), though the heavier tail makes it more seed-sensitive than in the undamped case. Damping therefore does not simplify the near-boundary prediction task, and we report this openly.
6.6. Conformal certification
The split-conformal interval achieves valid empirical coverage at every level (Neva = 178): 92.7% at the 95% target, 89.3% at 90%, 79.2% at 80%, and 49.4% at 50%, confirming that the bound is not anticonservative (Fig. 5). The same split calibration transfers to the multi-topology operators: on held-out N−1 fault buses and line trips the empirical coverage is 0.919 and 0.886, and on held-out N−2 buses and line pairs 0.850 and 0.950 (interval half-widths 108–196°; calibration/evaluation set sizes are given in the calibration appendix 〖V3：补各 split 的 N_cal/N_eval 与补充材料编号〗); deviations from the 0.90 target are within the sampling noise of the smaller evaluation sets. A Mondrian (grouped) calibration [22] by post-fault peak angle, at α = 0.1, shows that the interval half-width grows toward the stability boundary, from 81.2° for scenarios peaking below 45° to 140.9° for those peaking above 90° (with group sizes and per-group coverage 0.875/0.933/0.833 reported in Fig. 5〖V8〗): coverage is approximately nominal away from the boundary and slightly below nominal for the near-boundary group. The interval is therefore wide precisely where the stability decision is most critical; the bound is best understood as a conservative screening envelope that guarantees coverage, rather than a tight predictive interval.
The directly calibrated pairwise-difference score Rpw (11) certifies the pairwise angle difference—the exact quantity appearing in the stability criterion—to 102° at α = 0.1 (coverage 0.893), below the π threshold, making this score informative for the stability decision. By contrast, per-machine calibration with Bonferroni/Šidák multiplicity corrections does not tighten the bound (pairwise 617–621°): each machine’s worst-over-time score has heavy tails, so the α/n correction pushes the per-machine quantiles deep into the tail. Calibrating instead on the validation set (training-bus scenarios) and evaluating coverage on the held-out buses yields only 0.61 at the nominal 0.90 target: the held-out-bus distribution is genuinely harder, so calibrating on held-out scenarios is necessary rather than a leakage concern, provided future scenarios are exchangeable with the calibration set. For deployment, rolling calibration windows refreshed on recent contingencies and domain-shift-robust conformal variants are natural directions; the calibration does not transfer across operating points (the N−0-calibrated interval covers only 0.51 of stable 0.8-load scenarios and 0.72 at 0.9 load), consistent with the operating-point limitation of Section 7—the binding obstacle is the operating-point shift itself, not the calibration split.
We also examined alternative scores (details in a supplementary table 〖V3：补充材料编号待定，并列各 split 样本量〗): an amplitude-normalized (relative) score does not tighten the bound (a band of 133% of scenario amplitude, coverage 0.86); a first-swing-window score—the worst error within each machine’s first swing—certifies the first swing to 68.5° at α = 0.1 (coverage 0.904), notably tighter than the full-trajectory 111°, and it is the first swing that determines the CCT; a time-averaged score yields 47.6° with 0.899 coverage at the cost of bounding the time average rather than the uniform-in-time error. In the gated regime where the interval is applied only after a predicted-stable decision—the natural operational workflow, since the bound is only well-posed on stable trajectories—the gate is 97.9% pure and the empirical coverage within the gate is 0.946, higher than the marginal 0.893 because the gate excludes the hardest near-boundary cases; this gated coverage is an empirical observation rather than a formal guarantee, since conditioning on the prediction breaks exchangeability. Adaptive and locally adaptive conformal methods and conformalized quantile regression [24–26] are natural candidates for further reducing the near-boundary conservatism; time-percentile scores are left to future work.

6.7. The negative result: input encoding versus residual loss
The auxiliary physics-residual loss does not help even when correctly specified. An early implementation of the residual omitted the synchronous-speed factor ωs on the power-mismatch terms of (9); after correcting this scaling, the pooled stable-trajectory RMSE is 39.2° at λ = 0.001 (versus 36.2° for the data-only operator) and 76.0° at λ = 0.01 (Table 3). The negative result is therefore not a misspecification artifact: the correctly scaled residual provides no improvement at the mildest weight and remains harmful at larger weights. Denser collocation (every output time point instead of every fifth) likewise does not help (48.9° pooled, 92.9% accuracy), so the negative result is not an artifact of the collocation stride. We attribute this to the fact that, without the correct post-fault initial condition, the swing-equation residual is a weak constraint satisfied by many trajectories, so it adds regularization without information. Physics is therefore injected most effectively through the input encoding derived from the swing equation, rather than through a residual loss.
6.8. Near-boundary sensitivity and architecture ablations
To quantify how much of the trajectory error is intrinsic to the stability boundary, we train on boundary-aware data (clearing times sampled within [0.4, 1.2] × CCT per fault bus) and find the pooled post-fault RMSE rises from 36.8° (uniform) to 70.8° (boundary-aware), confirming that the near-boundary trajectory is inherently sensitive to the sharp stability bifurcation and that the conformal bound is essential rather than optional. Stratifying the test scenarios by their distance to the TDS CCT quantifies this: more than 100 ms inside the boundary the stable pooled RMSE is 19.9° with 5.0% misclassification; within 30 ms it rises to 70.0° with 6.5%; and within 10 ms the misclassification rate reaches 43% (n = 14; the exact 95% binomial interval is approximately 18–71%, so this last figure is descriptive). The sharpest decisions are the least reliable, which is precisely the regime the conformal layer is designed to flag. Replacing the branch network with self-attention over the machines does not improve accuracy: at p = 512 the attention branch attains 116.2° RMSE and 93.2% accuracy (versus 36.2° and 96.7%), consistent with the severity encoding already capturing the machine-interaction structure; a Fourier-neural-operator trunk likewise gives no improvement (47.4° RMSE, 96.0% accuracy; Table 3). This further supports the choice of a simple DeepONet with a physics-informed encoding as the appropriate architecture.
6.9. Robustness to measurement noise
The physics-informed encoding ai(k) = (Pmi − Peif)/Mi is itself computed from estimates of mechanical power, inertia, and fault-on electrical power, which carry measurement error. To assess robustness, we perturb the encoded acceleration vector with zero-mean Gaussian noise (standard deviation proportional to each machine’s scale) and re-evaluate the operator. At 5% noise the trajectory RMSE is unchanged (36.2°); at 10% it rises to 40.6°, and at 20% to 50.5°. Classification accuracy degrades gracefully, from 96.7% on the clean model〖V2〗 to 96.1% at 10% and 94.5% at 20% noise (the clean baseline here must match the seed-0 model of Table 2; an earlier draft reported 97.1% from a different run). The operator is therefore robust to moderate measurement noise in its input encoding.
6.10. Scalability to the IEEE 118-bus system
To probe scalability beyond a single test system, we evaluate the operator on the IEEE 118-bus system (54 machines, using the canonical dynamic parameters of the Demetriou et al. [27] modified test system), with 24 fault buses held out entirely from training (3760 training and 960 test scenarios, clearing times in [0.005, 0.2] s and the system-adapted CCT search interval of Section 4.5). The operator attains 95.0% classification accuracy and 118.8° stable-trajectory RMSE. The classification accuracy is comparable to the 39-bus result (96.7%), demonstrating that the physics-informed encoding and operator architecture scale to a system with 5.4× more generators (54 vs. 10) and 3.0× more buses (118 vs. 39). The higher trajectory RMSE reflects the larger state space and the much lower critical clearing times of the 118-bus system (0.008–0.135 s, versus 0.13–〖V1：39 节点上界取 0.58 还是 0.45〗 s for the 39-bus system〖V1〗), a consequence of its many low-inertia synchronous condensers and motors. The CCT error on the 24 held-out buses is 9.3 ms (max 39.8 ms). The split-conformal interval attains 0.926 coverage at α = 0.1, but with a half-width of 595°—far wider than the 39-bus bound and larger than the 180° stability threshold, so on the 118-bus system the interval validates the coverage mechanism but is vacuous for the stability decision; the directly calibrated pairwise score and richer calibration data are required before the bound is operationally useful at this scale. We report this limitation explicitly.
7. Discussion and Limitations
We report limitations honestly. First, the post-fault trajectory accuracy (36.8° pooled RMSE) is limited and concentrated near the stability boundary, where the classical model undergoes a sharp bifurcation and trajectories are inherently sensitive. Second, the conformal bound is calibrated on stable scenarios, so it certifies the trajectory only in the region where it is well-posed; the stability of near-boundary and unstable scenarios is conveyed through the classification and the CCT band rather than through a numeric trajectory bound; on the 118-bus system the per-machine bound is currently vacuous for decisions (Section 6.10). Third, governors, power-system stabilizers, and dynamic loads are not modeled, and extending to higher-fidelity differential-algebraic models and inverter-based resources is natural future work; the damped extension of Section 6 shows that the encoding transfers without modification, although damping does not simplify the near-boundary prediction task. Hybrid event-structured designs, which separate the fault-on and post-fault branches or trunks while retaining the physics-informed encoding, are another avenue for better capturing the discontinuity at clearing; we leave this to future work. Fourth, the pointwise MLP baseline is slightly worse than the operator at full data (43.1° versus 36.2° pooled, Table 3), and the operator is clearly more sample-efficient at low data (a ≈9° advantage at 10% of the training data), which is where fast surrogates matter most; the operator additionally provides continuous-time evaluation and the conformal interval. Fifth, the operator is trained at a fixed operating point and does not directly generalize to substantially different load levels: classification accuracy drops to 75% at 20% lower load, while at 20% higher load 〖V6：明确失稳口径——基载潮流无解 / 故障后无稳定平衡点 / 抽样故障全部失稳（给比例与样本数）〗; training across multiple load levels and including the load level in the encoding does not resolve this (a model trained on four load levels attains only 65% on a held-out level), because the severity-to-trajectory mapping is not smooth across operating points near the stability boundary. Operating-point generalization therefore remains an open challenge and the binding obstacle to deployment. Sixth, we model only bolted three-phase faults at buses; parametric fault impedance and faults along line corridors are left to future work, although the severity encoding is continuous in the fault admittance and can in principle represent non-bolted faults.
8. Conclusion
We presented a physics-informed neural operator for transient stability assessment that predicts the full post-fault rotor-angle trajectory and the critical clearing time, with a conformal certification layer providing a finite-sample, distribution-free error bound. The key enabler is a physics-informed input encoding—the swing-equation acceleration vector, extended by a post-fault severity vector—which lets the operator generalize to unseen fault locations and to N−1 and N−2 line-trip topologies. On the IEEE 39-bus system the operator runs about 700× faster than time-domain simulation on identical hardware, classifies stability with 96.7% accuracy, and estimates the CCT to within 12.3 ms, on par with the best classical direct criterion tested, while the conformal interval achieves valid 89.3% coverage and the directly calibrated pairwise bound is informative for the stability decision. We also reported a negative result showing that physics-informed input encoding is more effective than a physics-residual loss for this problem. The operator is more sample-efficient than a pointwise baseline at low data, transfers to one-axis, exciter, and damped generator models without loss of accuracy, and scales in classification accuracy to the IEEE 118-bus system, where trajectory-level certification remains an open issue. Together with the openly reported operating-point limitation, these results define a fast, uncertainty-aware screening tool for TSA and a clear agenda for its deployment.
